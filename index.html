Technology — Top Priority

Protecting the AMOC and the Gulf Stream

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) and its key component — the Gulf Stream — face the risk of slowdown or collapse due to freshwater runoff from the Greenland Ice Sheet. Although the total freshwater volume is small on an oceanic scale, it forms a light surface layer that disrupts heat exchange and ocean circulation.

Explore Watson River plan Learn how freshwater behaves

Our approach (brief)

Redirect small rivers, streams and fjord outflows into pipes that discharge at depths of 50–200 meters (exact depth depends on local hydrography). Freshwater released at depth rises turbulently, mixes with saline water, removes the sharp surface salinity gradient and prevents the formation of a persistent fresh lens that would block AMOC.

Technology — detailed explanation

Our project offers a fast, reliable, and cost-effective means to reduce the formation of a near-surface freshwater layer that disrupts large-scale Atlantic circulation.

Mechanism of disruption

Freshwater runoff from Greenland accumulates at the ocean surface because it is significantly less dense than seawater. This thin surface layer reduces vertical mixing and heat exchange, weakening the AMOC and the Gulf Stream.

Our solution

Convey freshwater in pipes and release it at depth. As the freshwater plume rises, it entrains large volumes of saline water, rapidly increasing salinity at the surface region and accelerating vertical mixing by wind and waves. The result: the surface fresh lens does not form or persists for a shorter time, allowing AMOC/Gulf Stream recovery over a few years.

Watson River

The Watson River discharges up to 7 cubic kilometers of water per year. It is similar to many Greenland rivers but among the largest — making it an ideal demonstration site for our approach.

Plan: anchor a pipe on the riverbank above sea level, carry the flow to approximately 100 meters depth, and release freshwater through specially designed outlets. As the freshwater plume rises, it will entrain massive volumes of saltwater from depth, producing a mixed column that reaches the surface with near-ocean salinity.

Estimated cost: tens of millions of dollars for a single large river deployment. Compared to the global consequences of a Gulf Stream collapse, this is a cost-effective prevention measure.

Freshwater: Rivers and Icebergs

Greenland will remain a significant source of freshwater to the ocean for years to come. Different freshwater sources behave differently and their contributions to forming a persistent freshwater surface layer are not equivalent.

Icebergs

Icebergs often dissolve rather than melt at the very surface. A thin layer of surface meltwater near the iceberg is rapidly mixed by ocean water; because the iceberg is cold and extensive, much of its melt mixes downward. Freshwater from icebergs is typically noticeable within ~100 meters of the berg and becomes negligible at ~500 meters.

Submarine discharge

Submarine outflows that enter the ocean at depths of hundreds of meters rise along ice faces, strongly entrain saltwater and nutrients from below, and promote vertical mixing. They do not create stable fresh lenses at the surface and are dispersed faster by waves and currents.

Surface rivers

Surface river outflows are fresh, often warmed to around +10 °C, and contain suspended sediments. They form stable surface layers that are relatively resistant to mixing, block solar penetration, remain warm and buoyant, and therefore are especially effective at disrupting warm surface waters carried by the Gulf Stream and AMOC.

Jakobshavn (example of a large challenge)

Jakobshavn is Greenland’s fastest glacier and one of the largest freshwater sources — reaching peak discharges up to several thousand tonnes per second during summer (not counting icebergs). Its outflow drains into Ilulissat Fjord and then Disko Bay.

Addressing the Jakobshavn outflow is a larger, more complex task than redirecting typical rivers. Possible approaches include pumping surface freshwater into deep pipes and releasing it at depth so it entrains saline water and returns to the surface with much higher salinity.

Energy estimate: peak power might approach ~100 MW to treat the largest summer discharges. While substantial, such systems are feasible using distributed power sources and are still competitive when compared to the cost of global climate disruption.

Note: these are preliminary calculations and concepts; each large deployment requires detailed local hydrographic and engineering studies.
